A village of converted shipping containers that travels Europe's rivers. Residents live on board, make crafts in a shared workshop, and trade with each town where the boat docks.

War

The story starts with the war: planes, tanks and people running.

The world argues

Europe and the aid agencies debate what to do. Humanitarian duty sits next to resource shortage, GDP growth and labor demand; culture, language and tax next to the risk of trafficking.

The camps

Where it lands is the camp: people held in one place, away from work and from the cities around them.

Why a village that moves

This proposal puts a small community on the water instead, so it can reach the towns along the river, earn its own way, and leave again.

The route

The boat follows the Rhine, Main and Danube from the North Sea to the Black Sea, stopping at thirteen cities on the way.

How it's built

Each module is a 40 ft shipping container, chosen because it is cheap, easy to find and simple enough for residents to convert themselves. The corrugated steel shell stays; insulation and an interior lining go inside so the box can be lived in through winter.

The container sits on a timber frame filled with plastic drums, which keep it afloat. Modules are then joined end to end.

How it bends

Two parts let the boat follow the river. Outside, accordion bellows like those on an articulated bus close the gap between modules. Inside, a turntable floor lets each module swing against the next.

When two boats moor side by side, doors in the walls open into a short passage, or an inflatable floating pad spans the gap. Each way of joining makes room for something different: a market, a laundry run, goods passed across, children playing on the pad.

Life on board

Six kinds of space share the boat: a dining hall, dormitories with curtained bunks, a workshop for sewing and packing, a children's area, a market room and a common room.

For each room I tested several layouts and kept one.

The round floors inside the joints become small extra rooms: fixed seating, a bar, a play area, or a screen where the living quarters need privacy.

Docking

How the boat meets a place depends on the size of the place. In a small town it moors at the bank and stalls go up on the shore. In a mid-sized port residents carry their goods further inland and drop off the boat's waste. In a large city the docks are crowded, so sellers head into the city to set up.

Who pays

A sponsoring organization funds the first containers and drums. Residents build and run the boats, and repay the loan from what they sell at each stop.

Reflection

Stop, don't stay. The boats dock but never settle. That was my answer to the question of cross-border status: residents trade in each city without claiming residence there. It still needs oversight, and the sponsor would carry it. Since the European Union and the UN agencies are the bodies that receive refugees, the sponsor would have to be one of them, responsible for river permits and special licences along the route.

Each stop is sized to its city. Small towns get a short stop for supplies and a quick market; large cities get several days.

What I would develop further. I drew the crafts from Ukrainian embroidery, because the residents I designed for were Ukrainian. Today I would not tie the boats to one nationality: what is made on board should come from whoever is living there. And winter: parts of the Danube can freeze in a hard season, so the route would run from spring to autumn, with the winter spent in a sheltered home port, repairing the boats and making goods for the next season.
